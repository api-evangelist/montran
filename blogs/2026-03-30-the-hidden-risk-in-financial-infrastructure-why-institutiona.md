<h3><strong>Introduction &#8211; Why Institutional Knowledge Is Critical</strong></h3>
<p>ISO 20022 migration, real-time settlement expansion, next-generation instruments, and AI-driven fraud frameworks are accelerating the modernization of payment and capital markets systems. At the same time, a critical gap is widening between technology deployment and the operational expertise required to sustain it.</p>
<p>Simultaneously, Central banks, private payment system operators, and their participating financial institutions are investing billions in next-generation infrastructure. In fact, the World Bank&#8217;s 2024 assessment highlights a key issue. Institutional capacity &#8211; the depth of technical and operational knowledge within an organization &#8211; remains the most cited risk factor in large-scale financial infrastructure projects. More importantly, the challenge is structural. Experienced operators are retiring. System complexity is increasing. The operational teams responsible for running mission-critical infrastructure must evolve as fast as the technology they manage.</p>
<h3><span class="discussion-id-327ea9fd-fc25-8001-8666-001cb2b8cca0 notion-enable-hover">Complexity Is Outpacing Capacity</span><!-- notionvc: 4a6f9773-56ea-4135-b2fc-a507e6535a6d --></h3>
<p>To understand the scale of this shift, modern payment and securities systems are no longer standalone platforms. They form interconnected ecosystems spanning RTGS, ACH, instant payments, exchanges, CCPs, CSDs, and cross-border corridors. Each operates under distinct regulatory frameworks and operational protocols.</p>
<p>For example, consider the scale of change underway:</p>
<h4 style="padding-left: 40px;"><strong>ISO 20022 Migration</strong></h4>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;">Swift&#8217;s ISO 20022 migration requires fundamental changes to message construction, validation, and exception management. These changes affect every every connected institution. As of November 2024, only 28% of global cross-border payments on the Swift network had transitioned to the new standard. By early January 2026, a few months <em><strong>after</strong></em> the MT-MX migration deadline, there will still 2900 out of approx. 7200 Swift users in scope who have yet to migrate, albeit representing well below 5% of payments traffic on the Swift network, according to the latest report shared with its national user communities). Critically, ISO 20022 is not an IT upgrade — every department from operations and compliance to customer service must understand the shift.</p>
<h4 style="padding-left: 40px;"><strong>24/7 RTGS Expansion</strong></h4>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;">24/7 RTGS expansion is increasing operational exposure in ways legacy shift models were never designed to handle. BIS/CPMI 2024 data shows that RTGS systems operate an average of <strong>66 hours per week</strong> out of 168 possible. Only <strong>five systems globally run 24/7</strong>. The share of central banks operating RTGS for 17–24 hours has risen to <strong>18.4%</strong>, with the Bank of England actively planning a phased move toward near-24/7 operation. Continuous settlement demands continuous operational readiness. This shift to 24-hour availability is also seen in securities, with the NYSE announcing their move to 24-hour trading for securities in tokenized form, possibly already going live in 2026.</p>
<h4 style="padding-left: 40px;"><strong>AI-driven Compliance and Fraud Frameworks</strong></h4>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;">These frameworks introduce new decision layers that operations teams must understand, calibrate, and govern. Deloitte&#8217;s 2026 Banking Outlook finds <strong>63% of finance functions actively using AI</strong>, with EY reporting that <strong>over 70% of banks are deploying agentic AI</strong> in some form. The institutions extracting value have teams that deeply understand these models. They can challenge, tune, and override them when needed.</p>
<p>Taken together, each of these shifts multiplies operational complexity. <strong>Complexity without corresponding capability creates systemic risk.</strong></p>
<blockquote><p>Infrastructure investment without proportional investment in human capital is an incomplete strategy. The institutions that operationalize new systems fastest are those that treat training as infrastructure &#8211; not overhead.</p></blockquote>

<h3>From Vendor Training to Strategic Capability Building<!-- notionvc: 1a2c6122-a5e6-42cf-a5c3-24114797b10e --><br />
<!-- notionvc: bb3a62d3-eee6-40eb-a1c4-9cdba0c66260 --></h3>
<p>As a result, traditional vendor training &#8211; focused narrowly on feature walkthroughs and system manuals &#8211; is no longer sufficient. Financial market infrastructures and institutions need structured, immersive capability-building. These programs bridge the gap between technology design and operational reality.</p>
<p>In practice, effective programs share several key characteristics:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Domain-integrated curriculum</strong>: Practitioners design training that reflects both the technology and the regulatory and market context in which it operates.</li>
<li><strong>Hands-on simulation</strong>: Real-world scenario exercises &#8211; settlement failures, liquidity stress events, fraud escalation &#8211; that build muscle memory for high-stakes operations.</li>
<li><strong>Cross-functional exposure</strong>: Programs that connect technology teams with compliance, risk, and business stakeholders to create shared operational language.</li>
<li><strong>Continuous development cycles</strong>: These programs go beyond one-time onboarding. They provide structured progression aligned with system evolution, business shifts, and regulatory change.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote><p>The most resilient financial institutions treat professional development as a risk mitigation strategy. When operational teams deeply understand the systems they run, incident response improves, compliance strengthens, and innovation accelerates.</p></blockquote>

<h3>The Institutional Knowledge Imperative<!-- notionvc: 8e3622fa-d8a5-4aba-8f8d-c08f666d0765 --><br />
<!-- notionvc: f758a4c6-0986-4b82-b6bf-bf127ddb7f6b --></h3>
<p>At the same time, the financial infrastructure sector faces a convergence of pressures. These pressures make make institutional knowledge more valuable—and more fragile—than ever.</p>
<h4 style="padding-left: 40px;"><strong>Generational Transition</strong></h4>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;">First, a generational transition is underway. Datos Insights reports that a critical mass of experienced operators who built and sustained legacy infrastructure are projected to retire by <strong>2027. </strong>They will take decades of tacit knowledge with them. Finance professional pipelines have contracted sharply, with CPA candidates <strong>down 27% over the past decade</strong>, compounding the IT talent shortage.</p>
<h4 style="padding-left: 40px;"><strong>Regulatory Acceleration</strong></h4>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;">Meanwhile, the EU&#8217;s Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) &#8211; effective January 2025 &#8211; is already creating compliance pressure, with <strong>only 8% of financial institutions</strong> reporting full compliance on third-party risk and resilience testing. From CPMI-IOSCO operational guidance to evolving CBPR+ market practice requirements, the compliance burden is now continuous, not episodic.</p>
<h4 style="padding-left: 40px;"><strong>Technology Convergence</strong></h4>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;">In parallel, AI, cloud, and DLT capabilities are converging into core systems, the operational skill set required is fundamentally changing. The institutions that thrive are those building teams capable of navigating this intersection &#8211; not just implementing tools, but understanding them deeply enough to manage, override, and evolve them.</p>
<blockquote><p>Institutional knowledge is not a static asset &#8211; it must be continuously renewed. Organizations that systematize capability building create durable competitive advantages that technology procurement alone cannot replicate.</p></blockquote>

<h3>Strategic Imperatives</h3>
<p>Given these challenges, institutions should focus on four strategic imperatives:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat training as infrastructure investment</strong>: Allocate capability building alongside technology budgets, not as an afterthought.</li>
<li><strong>Prioritize practitioner-led programs</strong>: Seek development partners with deep domain expertise and hands-on implementation experience across markets.</li>
<li><strong>Build continuous learning frameworks</strong>: Align professional development cycles with system upgrades, regulatory changes, and operational milestones.</li>
<li><strong>Make capability building a talent strategy</strong>: Structured professional development attracts stronger candidates and retains experienced operators — turning training into a competitive advantage.</li>
</ol>
<p><!--more--></p>
<p>Financial infrastructure is only as strong as the people who operate it. In an era of accelerating complexity, institutional knowledge is not a support function &#8211; it is a strategic asset.<!-- notionvc: 32a52cb4-bb9c-47a1-9fcf-8f9e4d3346b6 --></p>
